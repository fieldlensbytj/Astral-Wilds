# Astral Wilds: Canon Bible

Sep 18, 2026 · @Someone

## I. Core Identity & Central Question

Astral Wilds is a premium, single-player, open-world science-fantasy creature-taming action RPG about exploration, companionship, political intrigue, and the relationship between humanity and Astrals — creatures that manipulate a universal energy called Aether.

The player is a Mage, one of the rare humans with enough Resonance to bond with an Astral. Unlike traditional creature-battlers, the Mage fights personally alongside their team. The signature combat structure is:

Mage + 2 Astrals vs. Mage + 2 Astrals

The story begins as one about becoming a great Mage and gradually becomes one about discovering whether the entire civilization of Magi was built on a lie.

**Central question:**

Is a bond still a bond when one side controls the conditions?

This applies to Mage and Astral, ruler and citizen, House and servant, Empire and world, creator and artificial life, parent and heir, military and soldier, and civilization and nature. The core political conflict is between two extremes: control without consent vs. freedom without certainty. The Dominion is not simply evil — its systems were built for reasons, some selfish, some horrific, some possibly necessary to prevent another catastrophe.

## II. The Crowned Reach & The Celestial Dominion

Human civilization spans seven inhabited worlds called the Crowned Reach, linked by ancient Aether currents called Astral Routes. Ordinary space travel exists, but interstellar distances are enormous; Astral Routes let equipped starships cross them far faster. Travel access is political — the wealthy, Great Houses, and merchants move freely; an ordinary citizen may never leave their homeworld.

The dominant government is the Celestial Dominion, which oversees Mage licensing, interplanetary law, Astral bonding standards, the Astral Routes, major military forces, the Crown Circuit, Ascension, Aether research, and the Crown Network.

The Dominion is authoritarian but not incompetent — trade flourishes, cities prosper, piracy is controlled, and millions of citizens genuinely have better lives under it. Other worlds remember exploitation and conquest instead. This tension is meant to stay uncomfortable, never simple.

## III. Aether, the Aether World & the Ten Astral Essences

Aether is the universal energy field running through planets, stars, living organisms, ruins, and the Astral Routes. Astrals manipulate it naturally; most humans cannot. Humans with unusually strong Resonance can channel Aether through a bonded Astral — these are Magi.

The Aether World is a parallel energetic layer woven through physical reality, involved in bonding, recall, wand technology, Astral Routes, Convergence, and the Crown Network. Ancient Magi understood it far better than modern civilization does.

## IV. Astrals

Astrals are living creatures that manipulate Aether — not inherently weapons. Depending on culture, they may be wildlife, companions, sacred creatures, transportation, agricultural partners, predators, military assets, or research subjects. Intelligence ranges from animal-like to, in a handful of species, at or beyond human level.

Ecosystems should feel alive independent of the player: Astrals migrate, sleep, hunt, nest, form packs, compete for territory, and respond to weather. Some are active only during Aether storms, near ruins, or under specific celestial conditions.

There is no gacha system. Rarity is fully ecological — an Astral might be hard to find because it lives in an inaccessible biome, migrates seasonally, is nocturnal, requires a weather condition, occupies dangerous territory, is endangered, requires another species present, or only appears after a story event. The reward comes from learning the world, not from randomized pulls.

## V. The Astraldex & Evolution

The player's research system, the Astraldex, tracks more than ownership: typing, habitat, diet, behavior, evolution, planetary variants, migration, combat tendencies, and lore. Each entry progresses through Seen → Acquired → Researched → Fully Documented — receiving an Astral through the Aether Exchange registers the species but doesn't automatically reveal its wild ecology.

Scale target: ~400–450 core species, with planetary variants, alternate evolutions, ancient/Forged/Hollowed forms, and rare subspecies bringing the practical Astraldex to ~500–600 entries.

Evolution has four tracks, which can combine on a single species:

Standard Evolution — triggered by growth/level.

Resonant Evolution — shaped by local planetary Aether (a species evolved on Nocthyra may diverge from the same species raised on Astryra).

Bond Evolution — requires sufficient trust between Mage and Astral.

Conditional Evolution — triggered by specific environments, artifacts, celestial conditions, or achievements.

## VI. The Opening

The game does not begin with a professor handing the player a starter. On Astryra, the protagonist follows an abnormal Aether signal to an ancient crashed starship, fallen millennia ago, its architecture predating the Dominion and matching no modern House. Deep inside, one chamber still holds power: an Aether Tear — a wound between physical reality and the Aether World.

Through the Tear, the protagonist hears the First Astral, also called the Progenitor — the oldest known Astral, older than the Six Sovereigns and possibly older than recorded human civilization. It presents three young Astrals:

Each starter's secondary typing is deliberately withheld until final evolution, though earlier stages should foreshadow it (e.g., an eventually-Gale Cindrel shows unusually explosive aerial movement early on). The final form reveals something that was always present.

Whichever starter is chosen, its wand activates without the Crown Seal engaging — the Astral approaches voluntarily, and Mage and Astral form a natural bond through the Aether World: Old Concordance, believed lost for millennia. The Tear closes, leaving the protagonist and their starter together in the wreck; their first battle happens while escaping it. The wreck itself remains a recurring dungeon and archaeological mystery revisited throughout the saga, not a one-time tutorial location — see Section XII for its relationship to the player's later starship.

## VII. The Wand & The Resonance Weave

The Mage's tool is the Astral Conduit, commonly called a wand — replacing the sphere/card/capsule concept entirely. It handles Astral bonding, Mage combat, magical defense, Aether scanning, recall, environmental interaction, Convergence, and research. The wand grows alongside the player throughout the game.

**Wand customization has four configurable systems:**

Core — the wand's fundamental Aether signature; favors specific Essences or broader magical functions.

Focus — how magic is projected; shapes casting style, bond stability, range, targeting, resistance management.

Sigil — specialized effects (faster bond development, more research data, reduced reagent use, better healing, category-specific bonuses).

Frame — the Mage's combat identity. Locked to four clear archetypes rather than a loose spectrum — see "The Four Wand Frames" below.

There is no single universally superior wand — the objective is player build diversity across Core/Focus/Sigil/Frame combinations.

The Crown Seal: modern wands contain a hidden subsystem that can suppress portions of an Astral's natural resistance to bonding. Magi are taught the wand simply stabilizes a bond; almost none know the Seal exists. Its eventual discovery forces the protagonist — and the player — to question the entire culture of Astral capture. The player is politically dangerous not because they are immediately the strongest Mage alive, but because their starter's bond proves the existing system is unnecessary.

Old Concordance, by contrast, is reciprocal: the Astral chooses, the Mage chooses, and neither magically overrides the other. It predates the Crown system and was how ancient civilization bonded before the modern Dominion replaced it.

### The Resonance Weave (canonical bonding mechanic)

Bonding is an active system, not a hidden catch-percentage roll. Every wild Astral has a visible state — calm, wary, territorial, frightened, enraged, curious, and others — and the player must first create a genuine opening before bonding is even possible. How that's done depends on the species: combat may exhaust an aggressive predator, food may draw out a timid one, helping an injured Astral may lower its guard, environmental interaction may attract certain species, and highly intelligent Astrals may require a quest or a choice before they will even consider the player.

Once an Astral is receptive, the player raises the wand and initiates Resonance. An Aether tether forms between Mage and Astral, and the player actively stabilizes it through a short real-time interaction built around maintaining alignment and responding to resistance pulses — less "hit the timing zone," more balancing a magical connection: push too aggressively and the Astral rejects you; release too much pressure and the link collapses.

Difficulty is modified by the Astral's species, temperament, and remaining stamina; the Mage's wand Core/Focus/Sigil; specialized reagents; and prior interactions with that Astral. Underlying numeric values still exist, but they set the difficulty of the interaction rather than secretly deciding the outcome behind the scenes — if the player performs well, they succeed. A failed weave may cause the Astral to flee, become hostile again, or allow another attempt, depending on its personality. Legendary and unusually intelligent Astrals can break the normal rules completely.

This gives the game one repeatable mechanical language while still letting each species radically change the fiction around it — and it sets up one of the game's best mechanical reveals. Early on, the player may think they are simply getting better at "catching" Astrals. Later, once they learn about Crown Seals, they realize why the process has always felt subtly different when bonding through true Concordance. In Elyndra, an ancient Concordant Mage watches the player attempt a normal Crown-style bond and says, in effect:

"Why are you fighting its Resonance? Listen to it."

They teach an older version of the mechanic. The player is no longer trying to overpower resistance pulses — they are matching them. The exact same interface the player has used for dozens of hours quietly acquires a completely different meaning.

Specialized Aether reagents can be used for particularly difficult bonding attempts and are earned entirely through gameplay — there is no premium purchasing system.

### The Four Wand Frames (locked)

Wand Frames include real close-quarters combat, but not a separate weapon system. "Melee-channeling" means genuine close-range Mage combat — real attacks, dodges, combos, counters, and animation — but it does not mean the game needs swords, axes, spears, hammers, daggers, weapon loot, separate upgrade trees, or hundreds of weapon animations. The wand remains the weapon; a combat-oriented Frame simply changes how the Astral Conduit expresses Aether — projecting an Aether Edge, extending into a short staff-like weapon, or wrapping the Mage's hands and wand in condensed magical energy.

Core, Focus, and Sigil then modify what happens inside a given Frame — two Duelists can play very differently (a Gale Duelist might dash between enemies, a Terra Duelist create heavy Aether strikes and guards, an Umbral Duelist blink through shadows) while sharing the same fundamental Duelist animation architecture. That gives meaningful melee combat without turning Astral Wilds into a full weapon-based action RPG bolted onto a creature-taming game.

### The Resonance Weave, in full

The Weave combines three things at once — behavioral preparation, aim/alignment, and rhythm/timing — deliberately avoiding RNG, a long puzzle, or one quick-time event repeated thousands of times. An ordinary Astral's Weave should last roughly 4–8 seconds once the opportunity to bond exists; rare species run 8–15 seconds; only Sovereign, Titan, story, or highly intelligent encounters become longer multi-phase sequences.

Step 1 — Create receptiveness. Before the Weave can begin, the Astral must become Receptive, and how depends entirely on its species: a territorial predator might require combat first, a frightened Astral needs a slow approach, a hungry one accepts food, an injured one needs healing, an intelligent Astral may demand a completed task, and a social species may react differently depending on how its herd was treated. Once receptive, a subtle Aether symbol appears and the wand can initiate Resonance.

Step 2 — Enter Resonance. The player raises the wand; the camera tightens slightly on the targeted Astral. A translucent circular Resonance Sigil appears around the creature — not a detached full-screen minigame, the action stays visually connected to the world. Inside the Sigil are two moving patterns: the player's waveform and the Astral's waveform. The goal is to bring them into harmony.

Step 3 — Maintain alignment (Track). The Astral's Resonance point moves within the Sigil and the player follows it:

The physical loop stays intentionally simple — Track → Hold → Respond — the complexity comes entirely from the Astral's behavior, not from button count.

Step 4 — Resistance pulses. As the bond develops, the Astral's Aether pushes back: a visible pulse travels inward through the Sigil, and when it reaches the central harmonic ring the player presses Harmonize. A correctly timed press briefly synchronizes the two waveforms; a miss drops stability. Meanwhile the player is still tracking alignment — so the Weave asks for two simultaneous things: following the Astral's moving Resonance, and responding to its resistance rhythm. That combination should feel like communicating with something unstable, not throwing an object at it.

Step 5 — Stability. A Resonance Stability meter builds while alignment is maintained, resistance pulses are successfully harmonized, and the Astral stays receptive. When Stability reaches maximum, the two waveforms merge, the Astral's Sigil forms, and the bond completes — there is no second hidden roll that can still fail a full meter. If the player completes the Weave, they earned the bond; underlying stats change the difficulty of the interaction, never the outcome behind the scenes.

What the wand changes. A better Focus can widen the alignment tolerance; a specialized Core can make certain Essence patterns less erratic; a Sigil can slow Stability decay after a mistake; a reagent can soften resistance-pulse strength; high Concordance skill can make an Astral's waveform easier to read. The same Astral becomes genuinely easier to bond as the Mage's own build develops.

Species change the Weave, not the controls. A timid Astral's Resonance point darts away if alignment is pushed too aggressively; an aggressive Astral produces powerful, rapid pulses; a playful creature bounces its point around; a slow Terra species barely moves but generates very strong resistance waves; Volt Astrals move rapidly; Arcane Astrals may temporarily duplicate the Resonance point; Umbral species can partially obscure it; a genuinely intelligent Astral may deliberately vary its rhythm to test whether the Mage adapts. The player is, in effect, learning a creature's temperament through the act of bonding with it.

Failure isn't punishing. A failed Weave doesn't mean the Astral disappears forever by default — it reacts according to personality: it might flee, attack, become wary, need calming again, or simply allow another attempt. Some rare species may flee outright, raising the stakes of a high-value encounter, but ordinary encounters should never feel frustrating over one missed input.

Old Concordance changes the meaning, not the buttons. Modern Magi are taught to stabilize resistance — the Weave, as described above, is fundamentally about overpowering the Astral's pushback. Later, on Elyndra, an ancient Concordant Mage watches the player bond and explains that Magi once did something different: instead of forcing the Astral's waveform toward the Mage's, the Mage moves their own waveform toward the Astral's. The controls stay identical — Track, Hold, Harmonize — but the scoring and feedback flip: the player is no longer suppressing resistance pulses, they're matching them, the visual language turns smoother, and the sound design shifts from tension to harmony. A mechanic the player has performed for dozens of hours quietly reveals a second meaning: they thought bonding meant teaching an Astral to accept their Resonance; the old Magi learned to listen until both Resonances agreed.

## VIII. Combat — The Covenant of Two

Formal Mage combat follows an ancient rule, the Covenant of Two: a Mage may field no more than two active Astrals simultaneously in sanctioned combat. Maintaining more than two full combat Resonances at once is considered dangerously unstable — this is the player's unbroken combat identity for the entire game. Normal duels, Crown Circuit matches, Warden challenges, and rival battles are always:

Mage + Astral + Astral vs. Mage + Astral + Astral

The player never suddenly controls six Astrals or swaps into a different RPG battle system.

Magi participate physically in battle. The player can cast spells, attack enemy Magi, attack enemy Astrals, dodge, shield, heal, support, reposition allies, disrupt enemy Resonance, combine abilities, and protect their own Astrals. Team-building therefore has three parts, not one: Astral One, Astral Two, and the Mage's own build — your Astrals directly influence which abilities are available to your Mage, so two players with identical Mage builds but different Astral teams fight completely differently.

Convergence: deep bond levels unlock the game's signature ultimate system. For a few moments the Aether patterns of Mage and Astral overlap — the Mage does not literally transform into the Astral, but certain characteristics manifest through them. A Mage bonded with Gale/Volt Astrals might develop crackling wings of Aether and extreme mobility; an Ember Astral could surround the Mage in living flame; a Terra Astral could form stone armor around them; an Umbral Astral could allow short-range shadow movement. Dual-Astral combinations produce different Convergences — Cindrel + Runeclaw could produce a completely different Mage state than Cindrel + Mossling — which gives team-building another dimension entirely.

Format variety without breaking the Covenant: the world is allowed larger battles even though the player's own unit never grows past three characters (Mage + 2 Astrals). A late-story war encounter can place several Magi on the same battlefield — the player's Mage and two Astrals fighting beside an allied, AI-controlled Mage and their own two Astrals, while multiple enemy Magi operate elsewhere in the same arena — but every Mage, player included, still obeys the Covenant individually; allied Magi are simply AI-controlled characters with their own Astrals, not an expansion of the player's own cell. Titan and Sovereign encounters can invert the normal enemy structure entirely: it may become Mage + 2 Astrals against one enormous creature. A Warden gauntlet can force several consecutive Mage duels back-to-back with limited recovery between them. Scale and variety come from context, objectives, enemy structure, terrain, and allied NPCs — never from abandoning the Mage-plus-two identity itself.

## IX. The Seven Great Houses, the Crown Circuit & Wardens

Seven powerful Houses dominate much of Crowned Reach civilization. They are not elemental Houses — each controls a real institutional part of civilization:

House Aurelion currently holds the Astral Crown. Officially the Houses maintain balance; unofficially they compete constantly — through marriages, trade wars, assassinations, tournament sponsorships, resource disputes, control of Magi, proxy conflicts, and Astral breeding rights. All of it creates tension beneath the beautiful surface of society. This is the Red Rising-style aristocratic and political layer of the world.

The premier institution for Magi is the Crown Circuit — simultaneously a professional sport, a political institution, a military recruitment program, and a celebrity machine. Magi travel throughout their world completing Astral Trials; success earns Crown Crests, and enough Crests grants entry into major tournaments. Successful competitors gain rankings, and the greatest are invited into the Crown Ascendancy Tournament. Winning — or performing exceptionally well — may earn the player an invitation to Ascension (see Section X). The player initially believes reaching this tournament is the ultimate goal.

Elite regional Magi are known as Wardens — the game's equivalent of major progression bosses. They are not simply type specialists; each represents a philosophy, fighting style, or regional responsibility. A Warden could be a military commander, a naturalist, a noble, a clan leader, a scholar, a former Ascended Mage, an explorer, or an outlaw protecting a region. Their teams should feel carefully designed and tactically distinctive rather than generic gym-leader rosters.

### Warden legitimacy (locked)

Warden is an ancient title that predates both the Seven Houses and the Celestial Dominion — the Dominion later formalized the role because Wardens were already respected by local populations. There are three recognized kinds. Crown Wardens are officially sanctioned by the Dominion or sponsored by a Great House, overseeing major cities, Crown Circuit Trials, important ecosystems, and politically valuable regions; they can formally award Crown Crests and are the Wardens the player meets most often early on Astryra. Free Wardens operate independently — their authority comes from the people, local governments, clans, or longstanding tradition rather than a House; Velkara should have many of these, some cooperating with the Dominion and others deliberately keeping it at arm's length. Exiled Wardens were once recognized but have been stripped of status, declared criminals, or simply refused Dominion control — the government calls some of them outlaws, while local communities may still consider them their legitimate Warden; these become especially useful once the player's understanding of the Dominion changes.

That gives the term a deliberate arc: the player's first impression is Warden = respected official; by the middle of the game it becomes Warden = guardian of a place or people; and eventually, whether someone is legally recognized as a Warden and whether they deserve to be one are completely different questions. This fits Astral Wilds far better than making every major boss an employee of a House.

## X. Ascension & The Rivals

Every world knows the stories: Ascended Magi protect civilization beyond the ordinary boundaries of society. Their families receive wealth. Statues commemorate them. Children study them in school. Some supposedly defend distant planets; others fight terrible threats among the stars. But Ascended Magi almost never return. The public explanation is that their duty lies beyond their homeworld. The truth is more complicated — Ascension is partly an honor, partly military conscription, partly political indoctrination, and partly a centuries-long selective breeding program designed to identify humans capable of producing exceptionally powerful Resonance. Some Ascended Magi genuinely become elite protectors. Some become Dominion enforcers. Some disappear into research programs. Some stay loyal because the Dominion shows them dangers ordinary citizens never learn exist.

The truth should never simply be "Ascension is evil." It should be: Ascension is built on deception, but the threat it claims to protect civilization from may actually exist.

The rivals. Astral Wilds uses multiple recurring rivals rather than one Gary-style rival:

A childhood rival — emotionally closest to the protagonist.

A noble rival — raised inside House privilege.

A revolutionary rival — believes the protagonist is too moderate.

A Dominion loyalist — genuinely believes in the system and values stability.

An Astral liberationist — cares more about Astral freedom than human politics.

An off-world rival — challenges Astryran assumptions from outside.

They repeatedly cross paths across the 18 Acts. Early battles feel competitive; later ones become ideological. Someone the player defeated in a Crown tournament at hour 8 might be standing across from them aboard a Dominion warship at hour 70 — this is where the Red Rising-style character drama comes through.

The central rival travels the opposite road from the protagonist. Both begin wanting Ascension. Both discover the corruption underneath it. But where the protagonist decides the system has to change, the rival concludes:

"The system is terrible because the universe is worse."

They Ascend willingly. They become extraordinarily powerful, receive ancient Dominion training, bond with rare Astrals, and eventually become one of the principal commanders sent against the protagonist. They should not become cruel — they should still genuinely care about the protagonist, which is exactly why the rivalry works. Near the end they might say:

"You saw the cages. I saw what the cages were keeping out."

And for the first time, the player realizes their rival may know something they don't.

## XI. The Economy & Registry Rewards

The main interplanetary currency is the Mark. House Meridion regulates most major financial systems. Marks are earned through Crown Circuit victories, quests, research, Astraldex milestones, salvage, bounties, crafting, exploration, and trade.

Major expenses tie back to specific Houses, which keeps spending politically meaningful rather than neutral:

Wand components and starship upgrades — House Ferrane forges.

Ship fuel and travel permits / Route tolls — House Caelaris shipyards.

Astral medicine — House Lysenne.

Research and archive access — House Orienne.

Equipment, habitats, and cosmetics round out the rest of the spend.

Some worlds also use barter, local scrip, clan economies, or underground markets instead of, or alongside, Marks.

## XII. The Two Starships & Space Exploration

Astral Wilds has two distinct starships, serving two different purposes, and the distinction is thematically deliberate: the wreck represents the buried past, while the player's ship represents the future they are building.

The ancient wreck, from the opening (Section VI), is the millennia-old vessel connected to the Progenitor, the Aether Tear, Old Concordance, and eventually the deepest history of the First Light. It remains on Astryra as a recurring dungeon and archaeological mystery, revisited throughout the saga as new companions and technology allow the player to penetrate previously inaccessible sections. The player does not repair this ship and fly away in it.

The player's own ship, gained at the end of Act III, is a completely separate, fully functional vessel that the player names and customizes. It becomes the player's permanent home, crew hub, Astral sanctuary, and primary transportation for the rest of the game.

**Ship areas:**

Traveling should never be just a menu selection. The player walks onto the bridge, selects the planet, the crew prepares the ship, the Astral Route opens, and the player physically sees the vessel enter it — a journey that should still feel exciting fifty hours in.

Ship progression gates exploration and world access without arbitrary level walls: Drive System (real-space speed), Astral Gate Array (maximum Astral Route range), Aether Shielding (allows dangerous routes), Sensors (detect migrations, ruins, hidden signals, anomalies), Habitat Capacity (Astral storage/living space), Crew Capacity, Cargo, Landing Systems (operation on hostile planets), and Combat Systems. The game says "your current Gate Array cannot survive that route," never "you need Level 40."

In-system space exploration is not only a world-select menu. Players can explore within systems using the ship and encounter derelicts, asteroid fields, stations, smugglers, House patrols, Aether storms, space-adapted Astrals, distress signals, ancient structures, and rare resources. Astral Routes connect distant systems; normal engines handle local space.

## XIII. The Seven Worlds

### World I — Astryra, The Crown World (Acts I–III)

The cultural heart of the modern Mage tradition and the game's tutorial world — celestial castles overlooking forests, vineyards, and mountain valleys while starships drift above; ancient bridges between floating islands; Mage academies resembling fantasy universities; Astral sanctuaries in protected wilderness; magitech trains between great cities; orbital ports above medieval-looking capitals. All ten Essences appear here. World identity: high fantasy civilization hiding ancient science-fantasy infrastructure. Major city: Crownspire, capital of Astryra and headquarters of the Crown Circuit, built around the Astral Spire, beneath which lies technology far older than the city itself. Primary conflict: the player rises through the Crown Circuit while discovering elite competitors disappear after Ascension invitations. Sovereign myth: Astryrans teach that all Six Sovereigns appeared together at civilization's founding to grant humanity the right to bond Astrals — a story that is wrong.

### World II — Velkara, The Worldroot (Acts IV–V)

Enormous trees towering kilometers into the sky, whole ecosystems on their branches, floating islands over jungle oceans, Titan Astrals migrating across continents. Civilization exists mostly in elevated settlements built around enormous wildlife. Velkarans don't "capture" Astrals — they form Pacts, earning an Astral's recognition before it travels with them, which makes the modern wand immediately controversial here. Dominant Essences: Verdant, Terra, Tide, Ember (though all ten exist). Special ecology: Titan Astrals — not a separate rarity but species evolved to extraordinary size; some cannot be bonded at all, others require elaborate encounters rather than standard combat. Culture: community-based, clan-oriented, deeply suspicious of centralized authority; many Velkarans consider Astryra's capture system barbaric. Primary conflict: the Dominion is extracting enormous quantities of Aether-rich material from beneath ancient forests, and helping the local resistance puts the player directly against imperial forces for the first time. Sovereign connection: Velkaran mythology contains carvings of Terrakon older than Astryra's entire recorded civilization — which shouldn't be possible.

### World III — Nocthyra, The World Beneath Eternal Night (Acts VI–VIII)

Almost no conventional sunlight; life survives through geothermal energy, Aether, and enormous bioluminescent ecosystems — purple forests, silver oceans, glowing fungi, cities built into cavern walls, crystalline moons overhead. Dominant Essences: Umbral, Arcane, Frost, Gale. Culture: Nocthyrans believe consciousness itself generates Aether patterns; their Magi study dreams, memory, and synchronization, and this is the civilization that best understands Convergence — this world unlocks advanced Mage/Astral Resonance and true hybrid combat builds. Primary conflict: a mysterious organization is invading the dreams of powerful Magi — the player assumes an Umbral cult, but instead finds a network of former Ascended Magi trying to communicate without Dominion surveillance. Major revelation: some Ascended Magi are hundreds of years old (Aether exposure dramatically extends lifespan), several famous historical heroes from Astryra are still alive, and some willingly serve the Dominion. Sovereign connection: Nocthyra considers Nyxara the Keeper of Dreams; its oldest temples also depict Velcryos.

### World IV — Pyraxis, The Forge World (Acts IX–X)

Industry dominates the planet — volcanic ranges powering colossal Aether forges, megacities climbing mountains, orbital elevators connecting shipyards to the surface, districts built inside extinct volcanoes. Dominant Essences: Ember, Terra, Volt. Cultural identity: engineers, artisans, builders, labor guilds — Pyraxians see magic as something to be understood, improved, and engineered. This is where the player gets the deepest wand customization, starship upgrades, equipment crafting, Astral equipment, and Aether technology. Pyraxian researchers discovered how to grow synthetic bodies capable of housing Astral Aether patterns — Forged Astrals. "Forged" is not a new type; a Forged Astral can still be Volt/Terra, Ember/Arcane, or any other combination — it's an origin classification. Some are little more than drones; others demonstrate unmistakable self-awareness, raising the question: if humanity made an Astral, does it still deserve the same freedom as one born naturally? Primary conflict: House Ferrane and Dominion engineers are constructing an enormous military force of obedient Forged Astrals. Revelation: the technology inside modern Mage wands originated from this same research.

### World V — Elyndra, The First Garden (Acts XI–XIII)

Almost sacred in tone — white forests, crystal seas, floating ruins, massive bridges between mountains, ancient cities swallowed by gardens, wild Astrals displaying unusual intelligence. Dominant Essences: Radiant, Arcane, Gale, Verdant, Tide. The great discovery: Elyndra contains ruins predating the Celestial Dominion by thousands of years, depicting humans living beside Astrals with no containment sigils, no Crown Seals, no hierarchy — only Concordance, where Astral and Mage chose one another. The player discovers descendants of an ancient culture that intentionally withdrew from the rest of civilization and still practice Old Concordance; their Magi find modern capture wands horrifying. Primary conflict: the Dominion is desperately searching Elyndra for a structure called the Meridian Archive, which holds records from before the first interplanetary civilization collapsed. Sovereign revelation: all Six Sovereigns appear in its records, not as gods and not as weapons, but as Wardens who protected something called the First Light.

### World VI — Nhal / "Null," The Silent World (Acts XIV–XV)

"Null" isn't the world's original name — its historical name was Nhal, erased by the Dominion. Dead forests, empty oceans, collapsed cities, silent skies; almost no naturally occurring Astrals; Aether levels are nearly zero. Nhal was once a thriving civilization whose leaders rebelled against an early version of the Dominion; the response was experimental — the Empire activated a prototype of the Crown Network, extracted enormous quantities of planetary Aether, and the ecosystem collapsed, killing millions. Official history simply claims Nhal was destroyed by an Astral catastrophe. Surviving creatures, called Hollowed Astrals, are again not an eleventh type but a condition — they evolved to survive on almost no ambient Aether and can suppress magical abilities around themselves, which completely changes combat here: players who lean entirely on powerful elemental attacks suddenly have to rethink everything. Major revelation: the Dominion can perform the same process on any world in the Crowned Reach, including Astryra.

### World VII — Caelum, The Throneworld (Acts XVI–XVIII)

The heart of the Dominion, and it should feel almost incomprehensibly advanced compared with the player's home: planet-spanning cities, massive floating districts, artificial moons, vertical gardens hundreds of stories tall, Aether rivers flowing through transparent conduits, starship fleets filling the sky, Astrals imported from every civilization. The surface is beautiful, orderly, and prosperous — which makes the truth harder. Caelum isn't a miserable dystopia; millions of people live genuinely good lives there, forcing the player to confront a harder question: what happens to ordinary people if you destroy the system keeping this civilization alive?

## XIV. Mythology — The Six Sovereigns, the First Light & the Crown Network

The Six Sovereigns are the backbone of the game's mythology — singular beings, not rare species, appearing in archaeological records across worlds with no known contact between them:

Humans classified typings much later; the Sovereigns predate those classifications entirely, which is why they don't neatly represent all ten Essences — they embody natural cycles, not elemental categories.

Below them sit three pseudo-legendary species — exceptionally rare, but species rather than unique individuals, meaning players can actually raise one without diminishing the Sovereigns' uniqueness: Astravyr (Arcane/Gale), an extremely intelligent celestial dragon-like Astral historically associated with navigation and long-distance Aether travel — old Magi believed it could "hear" the Astral Routes; Gravemaw (Terra/Umbral), an ancient subterranean apex species living near concentrations of old Aether deposits, said to sense civilizations buried beneath the ground; and Aetherelle (Radiant/Frost), an extremely rare high-altitude Astral historically associated with healing, winter migrations, and sacred sites.

The First Light. Long before modern civilization, humanity encountered an enormous source of Aether buried within the Crowned Reach, called in the Meridian Archive the First Light. It may be an entity, a consciousness, a cosmic organism, the origin point of Astrals, or something humanity simply doesn't have language to describe — its nature should stay mysterious for most of the saga. The Six Sovereigns acted as its Wardens, and their purpose was never to protect humanity from the First Light — it was to maintain balance between it and the universe around it. Ancient humans attempted to control that power; the result nearly destroyed several worlds. The Sovereigns stopped them, and modern history was rewritten afterward. This event is remembered only in fragments as the Sundering.

The implied cosmological structure: the First Light → the First Astral / Progenitor → the Six Sovereigns → ancient Astral lineages → modern Astral life.

The Crown Network is not merely ceremonial — it is a massive system connecting modern wands, Crown Seals, Astral Routes, Ascension facilities, major Aether infrastructure, and military systems, giving the Dominion civilization-wide influence over Astral bonding. It also stabilizes certain dangerous Aether phenomena, so destroying it without understanding it could produce catastrophe. At the head of the Dominion sits the First Ascendant, a Mage who has survived for centuries through Aether, genuinely remembers previous catastrophes, and has watched civilizations collapse. Their argument is essentially: "Freedom nearly destroyed the worlds once. I will not allow it to happen again." They built peace through control; the protagonist's task is to challenge the assumption that control and chaos are the only two options.

## XV. Companions & The Aether Exchange

Because Astral Wilds is fundamentally single-player, companions become emotionally essential. Crew members are drawn from Astryra, Velkara, Nocthyra, Pyraxis, Elyndra, Nhal, and even the Dominion itself, and each has personal quests, their own evolving Astral teams, political opinions, relationships with other crew, ship routines, story reactions, and loyalty arcs. The ship gradually becomes a found family.

The ship should visually change as the player travels: Velkaran plants growing in hydroponics, Nocthyran crystals illuminating the observation deck, Pyraxian technology humming in the engine room, Elyndran murals decorating the archive, a piece of ruined Nhal resting behind glass, Astrals from seven worlds wandering expanded habitats, characters who once hated each other sharing meals. The ship becomes a tiny version of the future the protagonist is fighting for — different worlds living together without one controlling all the others.

The Aether Exchange launches as a fully offline, simulated system. It is Astral Wilds' one optional trading-style feature, in the spirit of a wonder trade: the player voluntarily sends an eligible Astral into the Aether World through public Aether Nexuses in certain settlements, and later through an Aether Chamber aboard the player's own ship. An Astral eventually emerges in return — the player does not choose what they receive. Mechanically and emotionally the system works exactly as originally envisioned, but at launch the source pool is a large curated population of simulated Magi and Astrals rather than real players, because a live version is the one feature that would force Astral Wilds to maintain server infrastructure indefinitely. Real-player Aether Exchange remains a possible future feature only if the game becomes successful enough to justify that infrastructure, and if added later it can replace or supplement the simulated pool without changing the underlying system. Most importantly, the game stays 100% functional decades later even if no server ever exists. The launch version of Astral Wilds is not called multiplayer at all — it launches as a completely single-player game.

Exchanged Astrals retain a limited generated Provenance: original world, original region, a Mage alias, number of prior Aether Exchanges, variant, and an original nickname where appropriate — creating the fiction that an Astral has traveled through other Magi over time. Players can also attach one Aether Letter from a curated set of prewritten messages ("Take good care of them," "We traveled far together," "May they guide your journey," "A gift from Astryra," "Good luck, Mage," "They love sweetfruit") — no unrestricted messaging is required or supported.

Restrictions: the Exchange is completely optional. There are no exchange-exclusive species, no exchange-exclusive forms, no trade evolutions required for completion, and no online-only achievements, cosmetics, or story content. The Six Sovereigns cannot be exchanged, and critical story Astrals cannot be exchanged while story restrictions apply. Only ordinary rare species may be exchanged.

### The permanent crew: 7 companions (locked)

Large enough to build a real found-family starship dynamic without producing an unmanageable amount of dialogue and companion content:

Five of the seven are Magi; two are non-Magi specialists — reinforcing that this civilization has important people who aren't Astral battlers at all. Wardens, rivals, Ascended Magi, and military officers can appear as temporary story guests, but they aren't full crew members and don't get the complete loyalty/relationship treatment.

Companion Magi obey the Covenant of Two exactly like the protagonist. A companion Mage can field Companion + Astral + Astral, the same as the player. In ordinary play the player still controls only their own three-character cell; in larger story battles, an allied companion Mage fights nearby with their own two Astrals under AI control. A late-story battlefield can therefore contain:

You + your two Astrals, and a Companion Mage + their two Astrals

against

Enemy Mage + two Astrals, and Enemy Mage + two Astrals

— the battle has grown from 3 versus 3 to 6 versus 6, but the Covenant itself hasn't changed at all. This is how Astral Wilds escalates from intimate duels into actual warfare.

## XVI. The 18-Act Saga

Crownfall. The final attack on Caelum brings together everyone the player has encountered — Velkaran Pact-Magi, Nocthyran Resonants, Pyraxian engineers, Elyndran Concordant Magi, survivors from Nhal, Astryran rebels, defecting Ascended warriors, former enemies, rivals, companions, and eventually the Sovereigns. In the central Crown chamber beneath Caelum, the player discovers the Crown isn't one object — it's a gigantic Aether network connecting all seven worlds, every modern wand, every Crown Seal, every Astral Route, every Ascension facility. The ultimate victory isn't blowing up the machine; simply destroying it would be catastrophic. It's finding a way to transform the network from control into connection — the same journey the player experienced personally.

The Final Astral Moment. Once the Crown Seal network is disabled, every magically compelled Astral bond across seven worlds is released — including the player's own. The Mage cannot command it, cannot recall it, cannot compel it. There is absolutely nothing forcing it to remain. The Astral looks at the player, then willingly approaches. Its Sigil reforms — not as a Crown Bond, but as Concordance. That is the thesis of the entire game delivered without needing a speech.

Then comes the last reveal. Everyone celebrates; the Dominion has fallen; the worlds begin negotiating a new alliance; the Houses lose their monopolies; Astral bonds become voluntary. Then all Six Sovereigns suddenly stop what they're doing and look toward the same point in the sky. Nyxara becomes agitated. Vaelstrix's feathers rise. Thaloros disappears beneath the sea. Terrakon begins moving. Solaryn takes flight. Velcryos awakens. The Meridian Archive activates by itself, and a translation appears:

WARD NETWORK: OFFLINE

**Then:**

EXTERNAL SIGNAL DETECTED

**And finally:**

THEY HEARD THE FIRST LIGHT.

The camera leaves Astryra, leaves the Crowned Reach, and travels into completely unknown space. Something unimaginably large changes direction — not toward Caelum, toward all seven worlds. Cut to black. End of the First Saga.

## XVII. Tone, Visual Identity & Product Pitch

Tone: Teen / 13+. The story can address authoritarianism, war, class inequality, ecological destruction, planetary genocide, death, artificial sentience, forced bonding, and political violence without relying on extreme gore. The game should be capable of becoming serious without losing its wonder — even after eighty hours of political conflict, the player should still be able to arrive on an unfamiliar moon, spot an unknown Astral in the distance, and immediately want to know: what is that? That joy is essential.

Visual identity: Celestial Science Fantasy. Aristocratic clothing, ornate magical wands, celestial runes, living magical creatures, ancient temples, starship docks beside castles, enchanted forests beneath orbital cities, enormous vessels crossing fantasy skylines, cosmic structures powered by magic. A Mage in an embroidered House cloak, carrying an ornate crystal wand, beside a giant magical Astral, while a kilometer-long starship passes overhead — that single image should communicate Astral Wilds. The world should never look completely medieval or completely technological; both belong together. The existing parchment-style Astral illustrations fit naturally as in-world field-guide plates created by Magi scholars, and the Astraldex UI can eventually render discovered creatures into that same illustrated-manuscript style, making the concept art canon rather than merely reference material.

Product identity. Astral Wilds should never be sold as "Pokémon in space." Its identity comes from ten things working together: a Mage who physically fights beside two Astrals; a monster-collecting journey spanning multiple fully realized planets; a customizable player starship that becomes a traveling home and ecosystem; creatures grounded in real planetary ecology rather than battle-only design; full interplanetary exploration; a wand that functions as both weapon and bonding system; a political science-fantasy narrative; a monster-taming story that interrogates the morality of its own bonding mechanic; a premium single-player business model; and a central relationship built on choosing rather than owning.

**The final pitch:**

Astral Wilds is a single-player science-fantasy creature-taming action RPG in which Magi fight alongside two bonded Astrals, explore vast alien ecosystems, customize magical wands, command personal starships, and travel between seven worlds governed by a civilization built upon Astral power.

After discovering a starship that crashed millennia ago, the player encounters a tear into the Aether World and hears the voice of the First Astral — the ancient progenitor of Astral life. It offers three young creatures: Cindrel, Mossling, and Ironbur. The bond formed that day is unlike any bond modern civilization understands.

What begins as a journey to become one of the greatest Magi in the Crown Circuit eventually reveals a hidden mechanism of control woven through every modern Astral bond, an ancient truth erased from history, and a conflict over whether civilization can survive without the chains it has mistaken for protection.

The emotional promise of Astral Wilds ultimately comes down to one moment:

When every magical chain disappears and your Astral is finally free to leave… will it still choose you?

## XVIII. Development Vision & Open Questions

The entire seven-world concept is the Astral Wilds Saga Bible — the canonical backbone (the ten Essences, the Six Sovereigns, the Seven Houses, the Crown Circuit, Ascension, the Covenant of Two, Concordance, the two starships) is locked, but actual production is staged rather than built all at once.

Vertical slice: one Astryra region; 15–25 Astrals; the three starters (Cindrel, Mossling, Ironbur); the ancient-wreck opening; one town; one Warden; one rival; basic wand customization; the Resonance Weave; Mage + 2 Astral combat; evolution; the Astraldex.

First major production goal: build all of Astryra and Acts I–III. This establishes the protagonist, the three starters, the Seven Houses, the Crown Circuit, the first Wardens, the main rivals, companion foundations, the initial Astraldex, the Ascension mystery, the player's own starship, and the first world transition. Only after those systems are fun and stable should development expand toward Velkara and the larger Reach.

Business model: premium single-player. No gacha. No battle pass. No subscription. No premium currency. No loot boxes. No pay-to-win. No paid Astrals. No MMO. No live-service progression. No FOMO design. The player buys the game once; everything required for 100% completion can be earned through gameplay. Future expansions or sequels may exist if justified, but they should add substantial new content rather than monetize systems already present.

**Open questions still to lock down:**

Whether Wardens are always affiliated with a House, or some operate independently or as outlaws.

Exact companion roster size, and whether companion Magi obey the Covenant of Two themselves during story battles.

Whether wand "Frame" orientations (melee-channeling, etc.) imply dedicated melee weapon combat and animation on top of spellcasting, which would affect art and animation scope.

The precise on-screen/input design of the Resonance Weave interaction itself — what "maintaining alignment and responding to resistance pulses" actually looks and feels like on mouse/keyboard and controller.

New open questions will be added below as they come up.
